Ramps

Allium tricoccum
Also called Ramps, Wild leeks

Ramps is a wild green you can forage from April to May in Northeast, Southeast and Midwest. Broad, smooth spring leaves in rich hardwood forests, with a strong onion-garlic smell and a reddish stem.

Caution. Overharvested. Cut one leaf per plant and leave the bulb; digging is banned in some parks.

When is ramps in season in the United States?

Ramps is usually in season April to May. Timing shifts with weather, latitude and altitude.

Where does ramps grow in the United States?

Ramps grows in Alabama, Arkansas, Connecticut, Delaware, District of Columbia, Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia, Wisconsin. Regions: Northeast, Southeast, Midwest.

How do you recognise ramps?

Broad, smooth spring leaves in rich hardwood forests, with a strong onion-garlic smell and a reddish stem.

How do you harvest ramps?

Cut a single leaf per plant with scissors and leave the bulb in the ground. Only pick from large, healthy patches.

How do you eat ramps?

Grilled, pickled, in eggs or pesto.

How do you store ramps?

Pickle the bulbs, freeze leaves as pesto.

What can be confused with ramps?

Lily of the valley (Convallaria majalis), rated deadly. Ramps and wild onions smell of onion or garlic. Lily of the valley smells of nothing when crushed. Lily of the valley has nodding white bell flowers in a row. False hellebore (Veratrum viride), rated deadly. False hellebore leaves are deeply pleated along their length. It grows from a tall stalk with many leaves; ramps have 2–3 leaves from the base. No onion smell.

What is ramps good for?

Vitamin A, Vitamin C.

What can you cook with ramps?

  • Pickled ramps: Ramp bulbs in a hot spiced vinegar brine.
  • Ramp butter: Chopped ramp leaves beaten into softened butter.
